Reading Other Drivers

⚡ Quick Tips

  • Most lane changes are telegraphed before the blinker — if there even is one
  • Big exits, lane drops, and construction ahead change how everyone around you drives
  • Bouncing side to side in their lane + randomly speeding up and slowing down = phone or distracted
  • If someone suddenly speeds up or slows down, they're about to do something
  • Brake lights show you who's nervous, aggressive, or about to do something unpredictable
  • Random blinker that goes nowhere, hesitation, speed swings — give them room and don't follow

Watch the Car, Not Just the Signal

Most lane changes are telegraphed before the blinker — if there even is one

▼

Don’t wait for a turn signal to know someone’s about to move over. Most drivers telegraph their lane change before they signal (or they never signal at all). Watch for the subtle signs: they start drifting toward one edge of their lane, their speed changes for no obvious reason, or their wheels start angling slightly. If a car is slowly creeping toward the left edge of their lane, they’re probably about to come over. Give yourself space and be ready.

Read the Road Ahead, Not Just the Car Ahead

Big exits, lane drops, and construction ahead change how everyone around you drives

▼

When a popular exit is coming up, the cars around you start behaving differently — even a mile before the exit. You’ll notice drivers in the middle and left lanes suddenly slowing down, checking mirrors more, or drifting right. They’re positioning for the exit. If you know the road, you can predict this. If you don’t, watch for exit signs and expect the shuffle. The 30 seconds before a major exit is when people make their most unpredictable moves.

Spot the Distracted Driver

Bouncing side to side in their lane + randomly speeding up and slowing down = phone or distracted

▼

Distracted drivers have a signature pattern. They bounce left to right within their lane — not a lane change, just constant drifting back and forth like a ping pong ball. Their speed is all over the place — 55, then 65, then back to 55 — with no traffic or hills to explain it. They react late to everything: green lights, brake lights, lane changes around them. Once you spot one, don’t hang next to them. Get ahead of them or fall back. Staying beside a distracted driver is one of the worst places you can be.

Speed Changes Tell a Story

If someone suddenly speeds up or slows down, they're about to do something

▼

A car that’s been cruising at 60 and suddenly accelerates to 70 is probably about to change lanes or pass someone. A car that randomly slows down near an exit is about to cut across lanes to make it. Speed changes without an obvious reason — no hill, no traffic ahead — mean the driver is about to make a move. Use those few seconds of warning to position yourself out of their way.

Taillights Tell You Everything

Brake lights show you who's nervous, aggressive, or about to do something unpredictable

▼

Taillights are the easiest thing to read and most people ignore them. Constant brake tapping in free-flowing traffic? That driver is nervous or distracted — give them space. Brake lights flashing on and off rapidly while they weave through heavy traffic for no reason? That’s an aggressive driver — keep extra stopping distance because they’ll cut in front of you eventually. A single long brake light when there’s nothing ahead? They’re probably about to turn or they’re lost. Reading taillights is basically reading someone’s driving personality in real time.

The Lost Driver

Random blinker that goes nowhere, hesitation, speed swings — give them room and don't follow

▼

Lost drivers are easy to spot: they’ll turn on their blinker, then not turn. They’ll slow way down, then speed back up. They brake at random intersections, stare at street signs, and make sudden last-second turns from the wrong lane. They’re not being reckless — they’re overwhelmed and making decisions a half-second too late. Don’t tailgate them, don’t honk, and definitely don’t assume their blinker means anything. Just give them room and pass when it’s safe.
